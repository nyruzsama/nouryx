Week 5 · General Science

Simple Machines

Lever · Wheel & Axle · Pulley · Inclined Plane · Wedge · Screw · Mechanical Advantage

Learning Outcomes
  • Identify and describe the six types of simple machines and their real-life applications.
  • Calculate Mechanical Advantage (MA) for each type of simple machine.
  • Distinguish between Ideal (IMA) and Actual Mechanical Advantage (AMA).
  • Explain how simple machines make work easier without reducing the total work done.
Key Concepts
What is a Simple Machine?

A simple machine is a mechanical device that changes the direction or magnitude of a force. It makes work easier by reducing the effort force needed — but does NOT reduce the total work done. The trade-off is always force vs. distance.

Work (W)
W = F × d. Measured in Joules (J). Work is the same with or without a simple machine.
Effort Force (FE)
The force applied by the person using the machine. Reduced by the machine.
Load Force (FL)
The force the machine overcomes (the weight being moved or lifted).
Mechanical Advantage (MA)
MA = FL / FE. If MA > 1, the machine multiplies force. If MA < 1, it multiplies speed.
IMA (Ideal)
Calculated from distances/dimensions; no friction assumed. Always ≥ AMA.
AMA (Actual)
Calculated from real measured forces; includes friction. Always ≤ IMA.
Core Formulas
MA = Load Force / Effort Force
Efficiency = (AMA / IMA) × 100%
Real machines: efficiency is always less than 100%
The Six Simple Machines
1. Lever — Three Classes

A lever is a rigid bar that pivots on a fixed point called the fulcrum. The relative positions of the fulcrum (F), effort (E), and load (L) determine the class.

1st Class Lever
Fulcrum in the middle. Can redirect AND multiply force. Ex: seesaw, crowbar, scissors, pliers.
2nd Class Lever
Load in the middle. Always MA > 1; always multiplies force. Ex: wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener.
3rd Class Lever
Effort in the middle. MA < 1; multiplies speed and range of motion. Ex: tweezers, fishing rod, baseball bat, broom.
Lever Mechanical Advantage
IMA = Effort Arm Length / Load Arm Length
AMA = Load Force / Effort Force
📌 Memory Aid
Remember positions by class: 1st: F-middle   2nd: L-middle   3rd: E-middle. The letter in the middle tells you the class.
2. Wheel and Axle  •  3. Pulley
Wheel and Axle
Two circular objects (different radii) sharing an axis. IMA = R_wheel / R_axle. Ex: doorknob, steering wheel, screwdriver, windmill.
Fixed Pulley
Attached to fixed support. Changes direction only. MA = 1. Ex: flagpole, well.
Movable Pulley
Attached to the load. Multiplies force. MA = 2. Ex: construction cranes.
Block & Tackle
Fixed + movable pulleys combined. MA = number of rope segments supporting the load.
4. Inclined Plane  •  5. Wedge  •  6. Screw
Inclined Plane
Flat sloped surface. IMA = Length / Height. Longer/gentler ramp = less effort needed. Ex: ramps, roads.
Wedge
Two inclined planes back-to-back. Converts downward force to sideways force. Ex: axe, knife, chisel, door stopper.
Screw
Inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. Finer threads = greater MA. Ex: bolt, jar lid, drill, spiral staircase.
📌 Key Relationship
The screw and wedge are both derived from the inclined plane. A longer/gentler ramp = greater IMA (less force, more distance). A screw with finer threads = greater IMA but requires more turns to move the same distance.
Must Memorize · Simple Machines
  • Simple Machine – changes direction or magnitude of force; does NOT reduce total work; reduces effort force.
  • MA = Load / Effort – MA > 1 = force multiplied; MA < 1 = speed/distance multiplied.
  • IMA – uses dimensions/distances; no friction assumed.
  • AMA – uses actual forces; always less than IMA due to friction.
  • Efficiency – (AMA / IMA) × 100%; always less than 100% for real machines.
  • 1st Class Lever – Fulcrum middle. Seesaw, crowbar, scissors. Redirects and can multiply force.
  • 2nd Class Lever – Load middle. Wheelbarrow, nutcracker. Always MA > 1.
  • 3rd Class Lever – Effort middle. Tweezers, fishing rod, broom. MA < 1; multiplies speed.
  • Wheel & Axle – IMA = R_wheel / R_axle. Ex: doorknob, steering wheel, screwdriver.
  • Fixed Pulley – MA = 1; changes direction only. Ex: flagpole.
  • Movable Pulley – MA = 2; multiplies force. Ex: construction crane.
  • Block & Tackle – MA = number of rope segments supporting the load.
  • Inclined Plane – IMA = Length / Height. Longer ramp = less force needed.
  • Wedge – Two inclined planes back-to-back. Converts downward to sideways force. Ex: axe, knife.
  • Screw – Inclined plane wrapped around cylinder. Finer threads = greater MA. Ex: bolt, jar lid.

Compound Machines

Two or More Simple Machines · Combined MA · Bicycles · Scissors · Can Opener

Learning Outcomes
  • Define a compound machine and distinguish it from a simple machine.
  • Identify simple machines within common compound machines.
  • Calculate combined MA; explain why compound machines are less efficient.
Compound Machines
Definition & Characteristics

A compound machine combines two or more simple machines where the output force of one becomes the input force of the next. This provides greater MA than any single component, but efficiency decreases because each part introduces additional friction.

Combined Mechanical Advantage
MA_total = MA1 × MA2 × MA3 × ...
Each added component increases MA but decreases efficiency.
Scissors
Two 1st class levers sharing a pivot (fulcrum) with wedge-shaped cutting blades.
Bicycle
Wheel & axle (wheels/gears) + levers (pedals & handlebars) + pulleys (chain) + screws (bolts).
Can Opener
Lever (handle) + wheel & axle (cutting wheel) + wedge (sharp blade).
Wheelbarrow
2nd class lever (body/handles) + wheel & axle (front wheel).
Crane
Pulley system + lever (boom arm) + wheel & axle (cable drum).
Stapler
Lever (arm) + wedge (staple tip that pierces the paper).
📌 Key Distinction
Simple Machine: one type only. Compound Machine: two or more simple machines combined. Compound machines are more powerful but less efficient due to accumulated friction.
Must Memorize · Compound Machines
  • Compound Machine – two or more simple machines; output of one = input of next.
  • Combined MA – MA_total = MA1 × MA2 × ... (product, not sum).
  • Less Efficient – more parts = more friction = more energy lost = lower efficiency.
  • Scissors – two 1st class levers + wedge blades.
  • Bicycle – wheel & axle + levers + pulleys/gears + screws.
  • Can Opener – lever + wheel & axle + wedge.
  • Wheelbarrow – 2nd class lever + wheel & axle.

Pascal's Principle & Hydraulic Systems

Blaise Pascal · Pressure Transmission · Hydraulic Lift · P = F/A · Fluid Mechanics

Learning Outcomes
  • State Pascal's Principle and explain pressure transmission in enclosed fluids.
  • Apply the pressure formula P = F/A to hydraulic system problems.
  • Identify and explain real-life applications of hydraulic systems.
Pascal's Principle
Statement & History

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was a French mathematician and physicist. He discovered that pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally and undiminished in all directions throughout that fluid. This principle is the scientific basis for all hydraulic systems.

📌 Pascal's Principle (Official Statement)
"Pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished in all directions throughout the fluid and to the walls of its container."
Pressure Formula
P = F / A    (Pressure = Force / Area)
P in Pascals (Pa) | F in Newtons (N) | A in square meters (m²)
1 Pascal = 1 N/m²
Pressure (P)
P = F/A. Force per unit area. Measured in Pascals (Pa = N/m²).
Force (F)
F = P × A. Push applied to a surface. Measured in Newtons (N).
Area (A)
A = F/P. Surface over which force is distributed. Measured in m².
Hydraulic Systems
How Hydraulic Systems Work

A hydraulic system uses Pascal's Principle with an enclosed incompressible fluid (oil). A small force applied to a small piston creates pressure transmitted to a large piston, producing a much larger output force.

Hydraulic System Equation
F1/A1 = F2/A2    (because P1 = P2)
F2 = F1 × (A2 / A1)
Larger output area = greater force multiplication.
📌 Example — Hydraulic Lift
A1 = 0.01 m², F1 = 100 N, A2 = 0.5 m²
P = 100 / 0.01 = 10,000 Pa
F2 = 10,000 × 0.5 = 5,000 N — a 50× force multiplication!
Hydraulic Brakes
Foot pedal transmits pressure through brake fluid to all four wheels simultaneously and equally.
Hydraulic Jack / Lift
Small piston force lifts a heavy car via a large output piston. Used in garages and service stations.
Hydraulic Press
Shapes metal and compresses materials in industrial manufacturing.
Excavators
Construction machinery uses hydraulic cylinders for powerful digging and lifting.
Airplane Landing Gear
Hydraulic actuators retract and extend landing gear smoothly and reliably.
Dentist Chair
Height adjustable via hydraulic pistons for smooth, controlled patient positioning.
Must Memorize · Pascal's Principle
  • Pascal's Principle – pressure on a confined fluid is transmitted equally and undiminished in all directions.
  • Blaise Pascal – French mathematician/physicist (1623–1662); discovered pressure transmission in fluids.
  • Pressure Formula – P = F / A. Measured in Pascals (Pa = N/m²).
  • 1 Pascal – equals 1 Newton per square meter (1 N/m²).
  • Hydraulic System – uses enclosed incompressible fluid to transmit and multiply force via Pascal's Principle.
  • Hydraulic Equation – F1/A1 = F2/A2 (P1 = P2). Larger area = greater output force.
  • Force Multiplication – F2 = F1 × (A2/A1). Piston area ratio determines force gain.
  • Incompressible Fluid – oil or water; cannot be compressed; required for hydraulic systems to work.
  • Hydraulic Brakes – pedal pressure → fluid → all four wheels equally and simultaneously.
  • Hydraulic Lift – small force on small piston → large force on large piston to lift heavy loads.

Archimedes' Principle & Buoyancy

Archimedes · Buoyant Force · Floating & Sinking · Density · Eureka!

Learning Outcomes
  • State Archimedes' Principle and define buoyant force.
  • Determine if an object will float or sink by comparing densities.
  • Calculate buoyant force using displaced fluid weight.
  • Explain applications: ships, submarines, hot air balloons.
Archimedes' Principle
Statement & History

Archimedes (287–212 BC) was an ancient Greek mathematician from Syracuse. He discovered his principle while stepping into a bath and noticing water overflow. He reportedly ran through the streets shouting "Eureka!" (Greek for "I have found it!").

📌 Archimedes' Principle (Official Statement)
"Any object completely or partially submerged in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object."
Buoyant Force Formula
FB = ρ_fluid × V_displaced × g
FB = Weight of displaced fluid = m_fluid × g
ρ = density (kg/m³) | V = volume (m³) | g = 9.8 m/s²
Floating, Sinking & Density
Float or Sink Rules
Density ConditionResultExamples
ρ_object < ρ_fluidObject FLOATSWood in water, ship (hollow hull)
ρ_object = ρ_fluidNEUTRAL BUOYANCYFish with swim bladder, hovering submarine
ρ_object > ρ_fluidObject SINKSRock in water, solid iron bar
📌 Why Ships Float
A steel ship floats because its hollow hull contains a large volume of air. The average density of (steel + air) is less than water. The ship displaces water equal in weight to the entire ship, so it floats. A solid steel block sinks because steel density (7,800 kg/m³) >> water density (1,000 kg/m³).
Ships & Boats
Hollow hulls lower average density below water. Buoyant force = ship weight when floating.
Submarines
Fill ballast tanks with water to sink (increase density); expel water to rise (decrease density).
Hot Air Balloon
Heated air inside is less dense than surrounding cool air; buoyant force pushes balloon upward.
Fish & Swim Bladder
Fish adjust gas volume in swim bladder to control buoyancy and hover at any depth.
Hydrometer
Instrument using Archimedes' Principle to measure the density of liquids.
Icebergs
Ice is less dense than water; ~90% is submerged, ~10% is visible above the surface.
Must Memorize · Archimedes' Principle
  • Archimedes' Principle – buoyant force = weight of fluid displaced by the object.
  • Archimedes – Greek mathematician (287–212 BC); discovered while bathing; said "Eureka!"
  • Buoyant Force (FB) – upward force exerted by fluid on a submerged or floating object.
  • Buoyant Force Formula – FB = ρ_fluid × V_displaced × g = Weight of displaced fluid.
  • Density (ρ) – mass per unit volume. ρ = m / V. Measured in kg/m³.
  • Float Condition – ρ_object < ρ_fluid; buoyant force ≥ weight of object.
  • Sink Condition – ρ_object > ρ_fluid; weight > buoyant force.
  • Neutral Buoyancy – ρ_object = ρ_fluid; object neither rises nor sinks; hovers.
  • Why Ships Float – hollow hull lowers average density below water; displaced water weight = ship weight.
  • Submarine – adds water to ballast tanks to sink; expels water to rise.
  • Hot Air Balloon – heated air is less dense; surrounding cooler air exerts upward buoyant force.
  • Density of Water – approximately 1,000 kg/m³. Objects denser than this sink in water.

⚡ One-Minute Final Review
General Science · Weeks 5–6

Simple Machines (W5): 6 types: Lever (3 classes), Wheel & Axle, Pulley, Inclined Plane, Wedge, Screw. MA = Load/Effort. IMA = dimensions; AMA = real forces. Efficiency = AMA/IMA × 100%. Machines reduce effort force, NOT total work.

Compound Machines (W5): Two or more simple machines combined. MA_total = MA1 × MA2 × ... More components = less efficiency. Scissors = levers + wedge. Bicycle = wheel & axle + levers + pulleys. Can opener = lever + wheel + wedge.

Pascal's Principle (W6): Pressure on confined fluid = transmitted equally everywhere. P = F/A (in Pascals). Hydraulic: F1/A1 = F2/A2. Larger piston = more force. Fluid must be enclosed and incompressible. Applications: brakes, hydraulic lifts, presses, excavators.

Archimedes' Principle (W6): FB = weight of displaced fluid = ρ_fluid × V × g. Float: ρ_obj < ρ_fluid. Sink: ρ_obj > ρ_fluid. Neutral: equal. Ships float (hollow hull). Submarines use ballast tanks. Hot air balloons: heated air less dense than surrounding air.
